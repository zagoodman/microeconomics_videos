\begin{abstract}
	We conduct a field experiment in intermediate microeconomics to examine the effect of introducing a small grade incentive to watch instructional videos on the exam performance of below-median students.
	Among nearly 400 students who scored below the median on the first midterm, we randomly assigned half to receive a 4\% grade incentive to watch videos.
	The 4\% grade weight was reallocated from the already-completed first midterm, so grade weights on the second midterm and final exam, our primary outcome measures, are identical across treatment arms.
	Students assigned to the incentive watched 63\% more unique videos than control students.
	We find no effect of the incentive on other observed study behaviors in the course or on grades in concurrent courses.
	Reduced-form estimates indicate that the incentive raised second midterm and final exam scores by approximately 0.18 standard deviations.
	Per-hour local average treatment effects are 0.16 to 0.18 standard deviations on the second midterm; final exam estimates are smaller, likely reflecting binge-watching near the deadline.
	We consider three models of student behavior, neoclassical, procrastination, and imperfect information, and find patterns most consistent with imperfect information.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

\epigraph{\textit{``You expect me to read the textbook? Ha!''}}{--- Anonymous student}\bigskip

Every year, university students spend thousands of dollars on tuition and course materials and hundreds of hours studying.
Yet many students struggle to learn: in economics, D/F/W rates are roughly 15\% in principles and intermediate courses \parencite{em2023, em2024}.
Instructors assign study tools, such as textbooks, problem sets, and instructional videos, but two questions arise.
First, which tools have a high marginal product per hour of student effort?
Second, can instructors improve outcomes for struggling students by incentivizing greater use of a productive study tool?
These are first-order questions for course design, yet the empirical literature offers little guidance \parencite{aws2015}, in part because both questions are difficult to answer empirically.
This paper addresses the second question directly and provides some evidence on the first.

To estimate the effect of a small grade incentive for watching instructional videos on exam performance, we conducted a field experiment in an undergraduate intermediate microeconomics course (Micro A) at a large, selective public research university over two academic years (2018 and 2019).
The instructional videos are part of the Intermediate Microeconomics Video Handbook (IMVH), comprising 220 short, professionally produced videos designed as an audiovisual analogue to a conventional course textbook.
Videos have a marginal financial cost near zero and can be accessed anywhere, which makes them a potentially scalable and equitable complement to classroom instruction.

Our sample consists of nearly 400 students who scored below the median on the first midterm exam.
These students have revealed, through their first exam performance, that their current approach to studying may not be working well.
We randomly assigned half of these students an incentive that reallocated 4 percent of their course grade from the already-completed first midterm to a forward-looking video-watching requirement: watch at least 40 of 48 eligible videos before the final exam.
Students in the control group faced no such reallocation, although the IMVH was freely available to all students throughout the course.
The grade-weights on the second midterm and final exam are the same across treatment arm and are our primary outcome measures.

The small grade incentive worked.
Students assigned to the incentive arm watched 63 percent more unique videos by the final exam, roughly 3.4 hours of additional content, than did their control peers.
Intent-to-treat (ITT) estimates indicate that the incentive raised second midterm and final exam scores by approximately 0.18 standard deviations.
Using treatment assignment as an instrument for video watching, we also estimate per-hour effects which require stronger assumptions that we examine in Section~\ref{subsubsec:LATE}.
Point estimates suggest that each additional hour of incentive-induced video content is associated with a 0.16 to 0.18 standard-deviation increase in second midterm scores.
Final exam estimates are smaller, at 0.04 to 0.05 standard deviations per hour.
The smaller final exam estimates likely reflect binge-watching near the deadline, making the second midterm LATE our preferred per-hour estimate.

We then examine whether the incentive affected other study behaviors, including lecture attendance, tutoring center visits, and course discussion board engagement, and find no statistically significant effects on any of these margins.
We also find no evidence that treatment reduced the number of courses taken concurrently or grades in those courses.
The lack of spillovers from treatment on any of these margins suggests that (1) the incentive operated primarily through increased video watching and the associated increase in study time and (2) the additional time spent watching videos came at least in part at the expense of non-academic activities.

We follow students to the next quarter, when many take the subsequent course in the microeconomics sequence (Micro B).
Treatment students were 7 to 8 percentage points less likely to enroll in Micro B (though not statistically significant), despite scoring higher on the second midterm and final exam.
This may reflect discouragement: by experimental design, treatment did not affect letter grades.
So treated students, who exerted more effort and performed better than the control group, saw no improvement in their course grade.
Among those who did enroll in Micro B, treated students watched significantly more videos despite facing no direct grade incentive to do so.
This result is robust to inverse probability weighting but generalized Lee bound confidence intervals include zero, so we view our evidence on the persistence of video watching as suggestive.
Point estimates for Micro B exam scores are positive but imprecisely estimated.

Our study makes several contributions.
First, we add to the causal evidence on the effects of incentivizing the adoption of video-based learning resources in higher education.
Unlike most prior studies, our design randomly assigns a grade incentive, allowing us to estimate the causal effect of the incentive on exam performance.
We also use treatment assignment as an instrument for video watching to estimate per-hour effects, though these estimates require stronger assumptions that we discuss in Section~\ref{subsubsec:LATE}.
A large interdisciplinary literature examines video-based learning in higher education.
A recent meta analysis finds an average effect size (Hedges' g) of 0.88 across randomized studies of supplemental video interventions \parencite{noetel2021}.
This estimate likely overstates the true effect of videos as most contributing studies do not hold total instructional time constant across treatment arms, with treated students getting additional instruction time through videos while control students receive no additional instruction.
Several studies address identification more carefully.
\textcite{fs2007} compare exam outcomes across years when students did and did not have access to recorded lectures, finding a positive but non-linear effect while \textcite{savage2009} compares two classes in the same semester, one with access to recorded lectures and the other without, finding no significant effect.
Both studies utilize across-class variation in access to videos but it is difficult to control for class-specific, time-varying factors that may impact student performance, including sample selection issues that arise when students choose classes based on unobserved characteristics.
\textcite{cl2012} use within-student variation, linking exam question performance to whether students watched the corresponding recorded lecture and find a 4 percentage point increase in exam question scores when the video is watched.
However, student fixed effects are unlikely to recover causal effects given substantial evidence that students adjust their study behavior following exam shocks \parencite{oettinger2002, ko2005, ss2008, bo2012, bo2015}.

Two recent studies use stronger research designs.
\textcite{artz2022live} conducts a within-class RCT in which students are randomly assigned to watch a recorded lecture on one of two topics while being excluded from the corresponding live lecture, with assignment reversed across topics.
They find positive effects of watching recorded lectures for one of the topics.
While isolating the format effect as in \textcite{artz2022live} informs how students should allocate time across study methods, the more immediate question for instructors is whether encouraging students to use a video resource improves learning, inclusive of any change in total study time that such encouragement induces.
Our design answers this latter question directly.
\textcite{aflsm2024} randomly assign access to two topic-specific modules containing videos, quizzes, and summaries, and estimate that access increases exam question performance by 0.14 standard deviations using student and question fixed effects.
Relative to their design, our study incentivizes engagement rather than providing access, generating exogenous variation in sustained video watching over the course rather than access to specific topics.

Second, we contribute to the research on how grade incentives affect student study behavior.
\footnote{See \textcite{sw2020} for a review of nonmonetary incentives, including grade weights, in higher education.}
A 4\% grade weight was sufficient to generate a large and lasting behavioral response among below-median students.
This contrasts with evidence that behavioral nudges, such as reminders, goal-setting and coaching, tend to have smaller effects on college study behavior \parencite{oppp2019, plg2020, pw2024}.
Our results are consistent with \textcite{afg2025}, who find a similarly large behavioral response to an 8\% grade-weight incentive for out-of-class quizzes in the same course the year prior to our experiment.

Third, our study contributes to a growing body of evidence that college students have imperfect information about the returns to studying.
\textcite{rc2023} provide direct evidence by first eliciting students' prior beliefs about the returns to study effort, finding that most students underestimate the returns.
When they randomly provide some students with information on the association between study effort and grades, students significantly increase their study hours and earn higher homework scores.
Our evidence is indirect.
In Section~\ref{sec:models} we consider three models of student behavior, a neoclassical model with complete information, a behavioral/procrastination model, and an imperfect information model, and examine testable implications that distinguish them.
Both the neoclassical and procrastination models predict that students will bunch at the incentive threshold and revert to baseline in subsequent courses when the incentive is removed.
We find patterns inconsistent with these predictions: treated students watched videos beyond the incentive threshold, and there is suggestive evidence that treated students continued watching more videos in a subsequent course with no direct grade incentive.
Our results are consistent with treatment causing students to learn that watching videos is an effective study strategy, although we cannot fully rule out habit formation.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows.
Section \ref{sec:studydesign} describes the study design.
Section \ref{sec:results} presents the results of the experiment, and Section \ref{sec:models} presents competing models of studying behavior.
Section \ref{sec:discussion} discusses our experimental design choices, their implications, and limitations of our study, and Section \ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Study Design} \label{sec:studydesign}

\subsection{Description of the sample and institution}\label{subsec:description}

We conducted the field experiment in an undergraduate intermediate microeconomics course taught during fall 2018 and fall 2019 by one of the authors.
The university is a large, diverse and selective public research university in the United States.
\footnote{The Carnegie Classification of Institutions of Higher Education classifies the university as an R1 (very high research activity) university.
	For the 2017-2018 academic year, the undergraduate student body had the following demographics: 49.1\% female, 50.6\% male; 75.0\% in-state, 5.5\% out-of-state, and 19.5\% international; 59\% students of color; 28.6\% majoring in the social sciences, 26\% of which major in Economics.
	Among newly admitted students, about one-third were transfer students.
	Average SAT scores were 652 and 605 for math and critical reading, respectively.
	About 34\% of students are the first in their family to attend a four-year university.
}
At this institution, intermediate microeconomics is a three-quarter sequence required for students majoring in Economics.
The experiment was conducted in the first course of the sequence, \textit{Micro A}.
We also observe grades and video watching in the second course of the sequence, \textit{Micro B}, during the winter 2019 and winter 2020 quarters.
The same instructor taught Micro B in both years (and was a different instructor than the one who taught Micro A).
Both Micro A and B instructors created half of the videos relevant to their course in the IMVH.

The class structure is similar across the Micro sequence.
Instructors teach two 80 minute lectures back-to-back on Tuesday and Thursday.
Students have the option to attend either lecture, and lectures are not recorded.
Two midterms and the cumulative final exam are held at a common time outside of lecture for the two classes.
In addition to lecture, students have access to a common class web site, weekly one-hour discussion sections run by graduate teaching assistants (TAs) who are all Economics PhD candidates, including, at the time, one of the authors.
In lieu of office hours, the graduate TAs and Undergraduate Instructional Assistants staff a tutoring lab open between three and four hours per day, six days per week.
Students may also attend weekly Supplemental Instruction (SI) sessions offered by undergraduates trained by the university in SI.
Besides the IMVH, students have access to a variety of online learning resources including a discussion board moderated by the instructional team, four years of previous exam questions, weekly ungraded problem sets, and semi-weekly graded online quizzes.
All course resources, including the IMVH, were accessible to students through the course web page.

Students were told about the experiment during the first lecture, given a printed copy of the consent form in the second lecture, and provided a virtual copy of the consent form in the syllabus on the course webpage.
At any time during the quarter, students could opt out of having their data included in the analysis.
\footnote{Students could opt out via an online form visible to a third party university organization so that neither the instructor nor research team could observe which students elected to opt out.}
Students below the age of 18 at the start of the course as well as students enrolled via the university's extension program were removed from the analysis dataset.\footnote{Students under the age of 18 were excluded per IRB protocol.
	We exclude extension students because of their potentially very different preparation for the course and our inability to observe pretreatment covariates and outcomes outside of Micro A.
}
Ultimately, four students under 18, five extension students, and seven students who opted-out were removed from the analysis dataset, leaving a sample of 850 students.

There are two unique demographic features of the class worth noting.
First, many non-economics majors take the class to either satisfy general education requirements or to explore majoring in economics.
As many students in the experiment are on the margin of majoring in economics, an important outcome is the likelihood the student takes Micro B.
Second, about 37\% of the class is transfer students.
For most transfer students, Micro A is their first upper division course at a four-year research university.
\footnote{Community colleges, the most common previous institution for transfer students, are on the semester system in the state of the university.}
We examine treatment effect heterogeneity to understand whether transfer students might differentially benefit from the IMVH.

\subsection{Description of the Intermediate Microeconomics Video Handbook (IMVH)}\label{subsec:handbook_description}

The IMVH is a collection of 220 short videos that cover the material in a year-long intermediate microeconomics course sequence.
\footnote{A preview of the IMVH can be found at \url{https://economics.ucsd.edu/undergraduate-program/resources/video-handbooks/index.html}.}
The videos were designed as a complement to both lectures and the course textbook.
Each topic typically has two videos, one with the graphical and verbal intuition and the other with the formal algebraic definitions and proofs, identified by having (Calculus) at the end of the video title.

The videos were created by six UC San Diego faculty members with professional videographer and production support.
Many videos utilize the ``learning glass,'' where instructors write with neon markers on a large sheet of glass that has lights embedded along the glass edge to make the colors pop.
The remaining videos feature faculty superimposed in front of slides that are written on during the presentation.
Videos are closed captioned and the captions were checked for accuracy by graduate students.
The IMVH was designed to help students find material quickly and so (a) videos can be accessed either via the table of contents or the index (b) for each video, the web interface includes time stamps for each concept covered, and (c) while watching the video, captions are searchable which allows students to jump to the searched-for word in the video.

While we do not know of another textbook completely comprised of videos, the IMVH is similar to the Khan Academy website, lecture capture, and textbook websites that incorporate instructional videos.

Besides the engaging viewable nature, the IMVH differs from a traditional textbook in that the instructors explain, graph, and derive mathematical results in much the same way one would in a conventional lecture.
Compared to lecture, students control the pace of the IMVH (they can rewatch, speed up, or slow down the videos), students can both listen to and read the IMVH due to closed captioning, and the IMVH has better visibility than many seats in large lecture halls.
Lecture has several advantages over the IMVH including that student questions can be immediately resolved, questions/answers have externalities for the learning of other students, and students can interact with each other before, during, and after lecture.
Compared to lecture capture, the IMVH can be used \textit{before} lecture to prepare and the IMVH videos are much shorter, focus on a single topic and exclude lecture components that do not record well, such as group work, student questions and class discussion.

\subsection{Experiment Design} \label{subsec:expdesign}

The experiment began four weeks into the ten week term following grading the first midterm exam.
All students who scored above the median on the first midterm, the \textit{Above median} arm, had a conventional grading scheme that placed weight only on exams and quizzes.
For students who scored below the median on the first midterm, we randomly assigned half to the \textit{Control} arm who had a conventional grading scheme that placed weight only on exams and quizzes (the same grading scheme as the students scoring above the median) and half to the \textit{Incentive} arm, whose grading scheme allots four percentage points conditional on watching at least 40 of 48 eligible videos in the IMVH.
\footnote{Watched in standard speed, 40 videos would require students to spend between 5.5 and 7.1 hours, depending on the length of videos chosen (on average 9.7 minutes in length each).
	Watching all 48 incentivized videos in standard speed would require just shy of eight hours.
}
These 48 videos cover new class content since the first midterm that would be assessed in the second midterm and final exam.
All students could still view the 26 videos relevant to the first midterm and, as they could help students on the cumulative final exam, we include them in our measures of video watching despite not counting towards the grade incentive.

The two different grading schemes are outlined in Table~\ref{tab:gradescheme}.
Notably, the four percentage points for video watching come at the expense of reduced weight placed on the first midterm score, which had already occurred at the time of treatment assignment.
Hence, at the time of treatment assignment, the video incentive is the sole forward-looking difference between treatment arms.

To improve balance between \textit{Incentive} and \textit{Control} arms and increase statistical power, we assigned students to treatment arms using paired randomization~\parencite{ai2017}, matching students by their first midterm scores before randomly assigning one member of each pair to \textit{Incentive} and the other to \textit{Control} (further details on treatment assignment can be found in Appendix~\ref{subsubsec:a_randomization}).
We emailed each student letting them know their assignment and grading scheme.
Students could also find their assignment listed in the online gradebook.
To confirm that students knew their assignment, we surveyed students using an in-class attendance quiz, and 94\% of students correctly identified their grading scheme.
We emailed the students who responded incorrectly to clarify their assignments.
\footnote{11 of 164 \textit{Incentive}, 23 of 167 \textit{Control}, and 10 of 373 \textit{Above median} students did not identify their grading schemes correctly. 146 students did not answer the quiz, several of whom had dropped the course following the first midterm.}

Students could leave the analysis sample in three ways.
First, per IRB requirement, students under the age of 18 at the start of the experiment were removed.
Second, students could anonymously opt out of having their data included at any time during the experiment.
The analysis sample was prepared and anonymized by a campus-based independent education research organization, which removed minors and opt-outs before returning the data to the research team.
Third, and most common, students could withdraw from the course.
Students may drop without penalty until Friday of the fourth week of the quarter.
Between the fourth and sixth weeks, a ``W'' grade is assigned in lieu of a letter grade, which does not affect GPA.
From the seventh week onward, students may no longer formally withdraw from courses.
In both years, the first midterm took place before the drop with no penalty deadline and treatment assignment occurred after the drop with no penalty deadline.
Some students took the exam and withdrew before finding out their midterm 1 grades.
Due to the lag between when a student drops and when the instructor is notified, we assigned treatment to several students not knowing they had already dropped the course.

We informed \textit{Incentive} students that they must watch the entire video and only one video at a time to get credit towards their 40 required videos.
It is not possible to observe ``watching'' as students could, for example, minimize their browser, walk away from their computer, or otherwise play a video without actively watching it.
As a proxy for watching, we use data recorded by the IMVH software that captures the video ID, student ID, and the date and time when a student opens a video link.
We define the following measures:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textit{Videos}: Number of links opened, including duplicates
	\item \textit{Unique videos}: Number of unique video links opened
	\item \textit{Hours of videos}: Total runtime of video links opened
	\item \textit{Hours of unique videos}: Total runtime of video links opened with duplicates removed
\end{enumerate}

For expositional ease, we use ``watching'' to refer to the link-opening behavior as defined above.
Although video watching in our data is a binary measure, watching behavior can vary greatly in intensity.
Some students take notes, pausing and rewatching portions of the video as needed.
Other students, we suspect, play videos in the background without absorbing much material.
Exploring the intensive margin of video watching remains an area for future research that will benefit from new technologies that can quantify video engagement including interactive content embedded in videos, eye-tracking devices, and more.

We helped students keep track of their progress towards 40 videos by periodically updating the online gradebook with counts determined from the IMVH data.
Although nearly all students followed our instructions to watch videos completely and sequentially,\footnote{The time between timestamps for video links opened in succession was almost always longer than the runtime of the video.
} in a few cases, students opened 40 or more video links within a few minutes.
We manually adjusted their video counts in the gradebook and emailed them a reminder of the requirements for videos to count towards the grade incentive.
\footnote{Although additional email communication as a result of treatment could violate the exclusion restriction, the small number of affected students is unlikely to have much (if any) influence on our results.}
Though it is doubtful these strategic students gained much from quickly opening so many videos, to maintain interpretability of our results, we do \textit{not} remove these clicks from our video count measures.\footnote{Our causal effects are per ``links opened'' rather than per ``links opened subject to certain qualifying conditions''.
	The cost of this interpretability is likely downward bias on our causal effect estimates.
}
Our \textit{unique} video measures are less sensitive to repeated opening of the same video, but are equally affected by rapid sequential opening of different videos, the behavior we observe among the small number of strategic students described above.
We rely primarily on unique video measures throughout, while noting that both measures are subject to this limitation for the affected students.
As a robustness check, Appendix Tables~\ref{tab:firststage_credit} and~\ref{tab:secondstage_credit} re-estimate Tables~\ref{tab:firststage_table} and~\ref{tab:secondstage_table} counting only credit-eligible video openings, i.e., excluding any video for which the student opened another video before it could have been watched in full.

To ensure fairness, we informed students that final letter grades would \textit{not} be affected by being in the experiment.
We accomplished parity between \textit{Control} and \textit{Incentive} arms through curving final letter grades.
First, we applied a curve to the \textit{Control} and \textit{Above median} arms as one group to achieve a final grade distribution in line with that of previous cohorts.
Second, we curved the \textit{Incentive} students' letter grades to match the letter grade distribution among the \textit{Control} students.
\footnote{If the top 4 percent of the control group received an A final grade and the next 6 percent of the control group received an A minus final grade, then the top 4 percent of the incentive group received an A final grade and the next 6 percent of the incentive group received an A minus final grade.
	We did this for each letter grade.
}

\subsection{Empirical strategies} \label{subsec:empiricalstrat}

We estimate the effect of being assigned to the \textit{Incentive} arm on our outcome variables of interest, Intent To Treat (ITT) effects, as well as the effect of watching videos for those induced by the incentive to watch more videos, a Local Average Treatment Effect (LATE)~\parencite{ir2015}.
The ITT is our primary causal estimate.
The LATE provides a useful per-hour rescaling of the ITT effect but requires stronger assumptions and is subject to several limitations that we discuss below.
We also explore whether there is treatment effect heterogeneity across key demographic variables.

\subsubsection{Intention To Treat (ITT)}
\label{subsubsec:ITT}
Our experiment has two-sided non-compliance: some students in the treatment arm do not watch videos and some students in the control arm watch videos.
Therefore, the causal effects of being assigned to the \textit{Incentive} arm on outcomes of interest, such as exam scores, are ITT estimates and not average treatment effect estimates.
Since the incentive itself in our setting is representative of how future instructors may induce their students to watch videos, the ITT estimates are policy-relevant for instructors considering a similar grade incentive to encourage use of video-delivered learning methods in their courses.

Our baseline ITT specification is the partially linear model:

\begin{equation} \label{eq:itt_spec}
	Y_i = \beta Z_i + f(X_i) + \epsilon_i
\end{equation}

where $Y_i$ is an outcome of interest (e.g.\ videos watched or test scores) for student $i$, $Z_i \in \{0,1\}$ is a treatment indicator with those in the \textit{Control} arm having $Z_i=0$ and those in the \textit{Incentive} arm having $Z_i=1$, $f()$ is a generic function through which $X_i$, a vector of controls, affects $Y_i$, and $\epsilon_i$ is an unobserved residual.
$\beta$, our parameter of interest, is the average causal effect of being assigned to the \textit{Incentive} arm on the outcome $Y$.\footnote{Our experiment takes place over two years, and we pool the sample across both years.
	Out of the 850 student-years in our baseline sample, one student repeated the course in both years, and hence there are 849 unique students.
	For simplicity, we drop the subscript $t$ from our specifications, treating the one repeating student as independent across years.
	Dropping this student from the sample leaves the results virtually unchanged.
}
Under unconfoundedness, $\wh{\beta}$ is an unbiased estimate of the ITT effect~\parencite{ir2015}.\footnote{Though we cannot test whether $Z_i$ is confounded by unobservable covariates, we have confidence unconfoundedness holds given the random assignment of $Z_i$ and the balance across observable covariates as demonstrated in Table~\ref{tab:balance_table_takers} and~\ref{tab:balance_table_matched}.}

In our baseline estimation of Equation~\ref{eq:itt_spec}, we include in $X_i$ a year indicator and first midterm score, following the advice of \textcite{bm2009} to control for all covariates used in seeking balance.
In a second model, we include additional controls chosen using the Post-Double-Selection (PDS) procedure of \textcite{bch2014a}, explained in detail in Appendix~\ref{subsubsec:a_selection}.
While we use heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors, these do not account for within-year correlation in the error term, which may arise because exam difficulty and teaching assistant grading standards are year-level variables.
The year indicator absorbs differences in means across years but not the within-year correlation in residuals.
Our next two estimation strategies mitigate this concern since estimation occurs within matched pairs that are formed within year and so absorb year-level variation.

In a third model, we fit Equation~\ref{eq:itt_spec} including pair fixed effects.
These fixed effects subsume the year indicator since pairs were formed within year, so we drop the year indicator but keep midterm 1 score to control for small differences within pairs along that dimension.
Because pair fixed effects require two observations per pair, we drop students who lost their matched partner due to attrition.

Our fourth model is a nonparametric estimate using Neyman's (\citeyear{neyman1923}) repeated sampling approach, considering each pair an independent, completely randomized experiment and averaging the results.
We estimate the ITT as the mean difference in outcomes across pairs:

\begin{equation} \label{eq:neyman_spec}
	\wh{\tau} = \frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J} \wh{\tau_j} = \frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J} y^\text{obs}_{j,I} - y^\text{obs}_{j,C}
\end{equation}

where $\wh{\tau}$ is the point estimate of the ITT, $J$ is the number of pairs in the sample, and $\wh{\tau_j} = y^{obs}_{j,I} - y^{obs}_{j,C}$ is the observed difference in outcome for pair $j$.
With only one unit per arm in each pair, within-pair variances are not directly estimable.
We therefore use the following estimator for the standard error of $\wh{\tau}$, which is conservative (yielding wider confidence intervals) if there is heterogeneity in the treatment effect \parencite{imai2008, ir2015, ai2017}:

\begin{equation} \label{eq:se_imai}
	\wh{SE}(\wh{\tau}) = \Big(\frac{1}{J(J-1)}\sum_{j=1}^{J} (\wh{\tau_j} - \wh{\tau})^2 \Big)^\frac{1}{2}
\end{equation}

As with the paired fixed effects model, this approach requires dropping students who lost their matched partner to attrition.
This reduction in sample size widens our confidence intervals, though the effect is modest because within-pair estimation substantially reduces residual variance.
We present estimates from all four models to demonstrate that the results are not sensitive to model specification.
In addition, the similarity of estimates across models that include all students and models that exclude students whose matched partner attrited suggests that attrition is largely unrelated to treatment status.
The similarity of estimates across models that do and do not absorb year-level variation suggests that within-year correlation of residuals does not materially affect our estimates.

\subsubsection{Local Average Treatment Effect (LATE)}
\label{subsubsec:LATE}
To estimate the per-video return to incentive-induced video watching, we use assignment to the \textit{Incentive} grade scheme as an instrument for videos watched.
Because watching videos takes time, and we find no evidence that treated students reduced other study activities, this estimate captures the combined effect of watching a video and the associated increase in total study time.
We estimate the following by two-stage least squares (2SLS):

\begin{equation} \label{eq:firststage_spec}
	v_i = \alpha Z_i + f(X_i) + e_i
\end{equation}
\begin{equation} \label{eq:secondstage_spec}
	Y_i = \gamma v_i + g(X_i) + u_i
\end{equation}

where $v_i$ is videos watched by student $i$, $Y_i$ is an outcome of interest (e.g., exam scores) for student $i$, $Z_i$ is assignment to the \textit{Incentive} grade scheme, $f()$ and $g()$ are generic functions through which pretreatment covariates $X_i$ affect $v_i$ and $Y_i$, $e_i$ and $u_i$ are unobserved residuals.
We estimate Equation~\ref{eq:secondstage_spec} by 2SLS, instrumenting $v_i$ with $Z_i$ using the first stage in Equation~\ref{eq:firststage_spec}.

Under the assumptions of unconfoundedness, monotonicity, excludability, and non-interference, $\wh{\gamma}$ is a consistent estimate of the LATE~\parencite{ai1995}.
Unconfoundedness is satisfied by random assignment.
\footnote{Although we randomly assigned treatment, one concern is nonrandom attrition.
	In the results section, we show that the \textit{Incentive} and \textit{Control} arms remain balanced across observables by the end of the experiment.
	Additionally, we find our results are similar when restricting the sample to students whose matched pair did not attrite.
}
We assume monotonicity: no student watches fewer videos because of assignment to the Incentive arm, that is, $v_i(1) \geq v_i(0)$ for all $i$, where $v_i(z)$ denotes the number of videos student $i$ would watch under assignment $z$.
A testable implication of monotonicity is that the cumulative distribution function of videos watched for each treatment arm should not cross and Figure \ref{fig:combo_cdf} shows that the two CDFs do not cross.
Under monotonicity, $\gamma$ is the per-video average treatment effect, local to students induced by the incentive to watch additional videos.
The population-average first stage, $\bar{v}_I - \bar{v}_C$, reflects the mean increase in videos across all students, including those whose watching is unaffected by assignment.
The per-complier increase in video watching is larger than $\bar{v}_I - \bar{v}_C$ to the extent that some students would watch the same number of videos regardless of assignment.

We have some concerns about excludability and non-interference.
Non-interference requires that each student's outcome depends only on their own treatment status.
This could be violated through spillovers between treated and control students in the same class.
Assigning treatment at the class level would address this concern, but would have required far more classes than our resources allowed.
We hypothesize that spillovers likely bias our estimates \textit{downward} as control students are more likely to benefit from having well-studied peers than to be harmed by, for example, peers too busy watching videos to join a study group.
\footnote{Although spillovers are possible, we believe their magnitude is likely small given that students have for the most part not yet formed strong social networks. 47\% of students in the \textit{Incentive} or \textit{Control} arms are transfer students in their first term at the university.
	The remaining students are predominantly sophomores taking their first upper division course.
	Social dynamics at the university facilitate networks within ``colleges'' more than majors for the very reason of encouraging academic diversity among peer groups.
	One example of a possible positive spillover is the online discussion board where students could ask questions about content covered in the IMVH.
}

Excludability requires that the incentive affects outcomes only through video watching.
This would be violated if, for example, \textit{Incentive} students reduced time on other study activities to watch videos.
In Table~\ref{tab:spillover_studying}, we show that treatment did not significantly affect observed study activities (class attendance, tutoring lab attendance, and use of the class discussion board).
We proceed under the assumption that unobserved study methods were similarly not affected by treatment.
A further threat to excludability is that the grade reallocation gave treated students an additional 4 percentage points tied to a low-uncertainty, time-consuming activity after the first midterm, which could affect motivation or effort independently of any learning from videos.
To shed some light on this issue, we note that the mechanical grade gain from reallocation ranges narrowly from approximately 2 to 4 percentage points depending on midterm 1 score.
If grade restructuring were driving our results, we would expect larger effects for students with lower midterm 1 scores, since they gain more from shifting weight away from their low score.
However, as shown in Table~\ref{tab:het_table}, we find no significant heterogeneity by first midterm score.
Nevertheless, we cannot fully rule out this channel for unobserved margins.
Since other study activities did not decline and watching videos takes time, \textit{Incentive} students likely increased their total study time.
Our LATEs therefore capture the effect of watching videos \textit{and} spending more time studying.

Note that our LATE is estimated in terms of hours of unique content, which implicitly assumes that all video hours are equally productive.
This assumption is unlikely to hold: videos differ in length, content and difficulty, and an hour of video watched steadily throughout the term is plausibly more effective than an hour watching in a binge before an exam.
To the extent that the \textit{Incentive} induces video watching that is less productive per hour than average, the LATE will understate the per-hour effect of video watching under more productive viewing patterns.

As with the ITT, we estimate Equation~\ref{eq:secondstage_spec} with four specifications: (1) year and first midterm score only, (2) difference in means across matched pairs and standard errors calculated using repeated sampling framework of \parencite{neyman1923} (derived in Appendix~\ref{subsec:a_neyman_late}) (3) adding PDS-selected controls and (4) adding PDS-selected controls with pair fixed effects.

\subsubsection{Treatment Effect Heterogeneity}

We investigate whether treatment effects vary along key demographic and pretreatment variables by adding an interaction term to Equation~\ref{eq:itt_spec}:

\begin{equation} \label{eq:het_spec}
	Y_i = \beta_1 Z_i + \beta_2 Z_i x_i + f(X_i) + \epsilon_i
\end{equation}

where $x_i$ is the demographic or pretreatment variable of interest.
When $x_i$ is binary (e.g., transfer status), $\beta_2$ represents the difference in treatment effects for those with and without that demographic characteristic.
When $x_i$ is continuous (e.g., pretreatment videos) $\beta_2$ represents the change in the treatment effect per one-unit increase in $x_i$.
We include in $X_i$ dummies for year and the demographic characteristic of interest as well as first midterm score.
Given our sample sizes, we are underpowered to detect reasonable effect sizes after adjusting for multiplicity.
We therefore focus on subgroups we hypothesized \textit{ex ante} may have treatment heterogeneity: our blocking variables (year and midterm 1 score), pretreatment videos watching, transfer status, and underrepresented minority status.
Pretreatment video watching may predict heterogeneity in either direction: students with more experience may benefit more from videos, but they may also watch many videos even without the incentive, leaving less room for treatment to shift behavior.
Transfer students are in their first term at a four-year university and may not have optimized their study practices.
The achievement gap between underrepresented and majority groups is well documented at the university level ~\parencite{bcm2009}, and it is important to understand whether grade incentives narrow or widen this gap.

\section{Results} \label{sec:results}

In this section, we first examine attrition and establish that the \textit{Incentive} and \textit{Control} arms in our analysis sample are balanced on observable characteristics.
Second, we show that the grade encouragement worked: students in the \textit{Incentive} arm watched significantly more videos than did their \textit{Control} peers.
Third, we estimate the effects of being assigned to the \textit{Incentive} arm on exam scores (ITT) and the per-video LATE.
The LATE likely reflects the combined effect of video watching and an associated increase in total study time (see Section~\ref{subsec:empiricalstrat}).
Fourth, to better understand mechanisms, we examine spillovers to other studying methods for Micro A as well as grades in other courses taken during the experiment term.
Finally, we see whether behavior change persists after exogenous incentives are removed by estimating treatment effects on video watching and exam scores in the subsequent microeconomics course, Micro B.

\subsection{Attrition and balance} \label{subsec:attrition}

High attrition is not problematic for this study, other than reducing statistical power, as long as attrition is independent of potential outcomes.
If attrition is influenced by treatment status, then the resulting nonrandom selection into our analysis could bias the results.
We show attrition at each stage of the experiment in Figure~\ref{fig:attritchart}, and separately for each year, within which treatment was randomized, in Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:attritchart_year}.
After removing minors and opt-outs per IRB requirement, our sample consists of 215 \textit{Control} and 215 textit{Incentive} students.
\footnote{In total, five \textit{Above median}, three \textit{Control}, and three \textit{Incentive} students were removed for age or opting-out.}
Thirty students dropped the course before learning their treatment assignment --- two \textit{Above median}, 19 \textit{Control} and nine \textit{Incentive} --- and additional students attrited before the second midterm and final exam.
The p-values in Figure~\ref{fig:attritchart} are calculated using two-sample t-tests of the equality of attrition rates between arms.
We find no statistically significant difference in attrition before the second midterm and final exams.
However, we do find a significant difference in enrollment rates in Micro B in the quarter following the experiment: treatment students were 9.7 percentage points \textit{less} likely to enroll in Micro B the next quarter than were control students.
Given the potential for selection bias, we perform several robustness checks when exploring the effect of treatment on Micro B outcomes in Section~\ref{subsec:spillovers_micro_b}.

Appendix Table~\ref{tab:exam_stats_by_year} reports the mean, median, and standard deviation of raw scores on each exam by year and arm.
As can be seen in Appendix Tables~\ref{tab:balance_table_takers} and~\ref{tab:balance_table_matched} (and by year in Appendix Tables~\ref{tab:balance_table_takers_2018} and~\ref{tab:balance_table_takers_2019}), we find no statistically significant difference between the \textit{Control} and \textit{Incentive} arms in observable covariates including first midterm score, year, previous term's cumulative GPA, videos watched before the first midterm, ethnicity, gender, and transfer status.
To correct for potential imbalance and to improve precision, we estimate models that include controls chosen via the post-double-selection method of \textcite{bch2014a}.

\subsection{Relevancy of the encouragement instrument}\label{subsec:relevancy}

In Table~\ref{tab:firststage_table} we present estimates of the effect of treatment on video watching.
By the second midterm exam, being assigned to the \textit{Incentive} arm induces students to watch 6.0 - 6.8 more unique videos than being assigned to the \textit{Control} arm.
The gap grows by the final exam to 20.5 - 21.6 unique videos, unsurprisingly given that the deadline to earn the grade incentive was the day before the final exam.
Following the recommendations of \textcite{ass2019}, we assess the strength of our instrument using the effective F-statistic of \textcite{op2013}.
The effective F-statistics for the second midterm and final exam first-stage specifications are 18.6 and 194.6, respectively.
The second midterm F-statistic is above conventional thresholds but is not overwhelmingly large so we also report Anderson-Rubin confidence sets in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:arci_table}, which are valid regardless of instrument strength.
These are similar to the 2SLS confidence intervals in Table~\ref{tab:secondstage_table}.
Figure~\ref{fig:combo_cdf} shows that the gap between treatment and control distributions is positive at every level of video watching by the final exam, with the difference most pronounced near the 40-video threshold and diminishing thereafter.
Figure~\ref{fig:timeseries} confirms that video watching was similar across arms before treatment and diverged sharply afterward.
Taken together, these results establish that assignment to the \textit{Incentive} arm was effective in inducing additional video watching, providing the variation that underlies both our ITT and LATE estimates.

\subsection{Effects on exam scores}\label{subsec:effects}

Table~\ref{tab:secondstage_table} presents estimates of the effects of treatment on second-midterm and final exam scores.
Across our four specifications, we estimate ITT effects of 0.17 - 0.18 standard deviations on the second midterm.
These estimates, along with our first-stage estimates (Table~\ref{tab:firststage_table}), imply second midterm LATEs of 0.26 - 0.30 standard deviations per 10 unique videos, or 0.16 - 0.18 standard deviations per hour of unique content.

For the final exam, we estimate similar ITT effects of 0.14 - 0.18 standard deviations.
However, given the larger first stage effects for the final exam, we estimate smaller LATEs: 0.08 - 0.09 standard deviations per 10 unique videos, or 0.04 - 0.05 standard deviations per hour of unique content.
The smaller final exam LATEs are consistent with the compositional concerns discussed in Section~\ref{subsec:empiricalstrat}: the additional videos induced by the instrument before the final exam are disproportionately binge-watched (see Figure~\ref{fig:hist_binge}), attenuating the per-hour effect relative to the second midterm.
For this reason, we view the second midterm as our preferred LATE, as it is less affected by binge-watching and compositional concerns.

\subsection{Spillovers to concurrent courses}\label{subsec:spillovers}

If treatment caused students to reallocate study time from other courses to watch videos in Micro A, we would expect to see a decline in grades in other courses, a reduction in courses completed, or a shift away from taking courses for a letter grade.
Table~\ref{tab:spillover_grades} presents estimates of the effect of treatment on term GPA, number of classes passed, and the fraction of courses taken for a letter grade.
We estimate effects separately for all classes, all classes excluding Micro A, non-economics classes, and economics classes excluding Micro A, since video watching in Micro A could improve performance in other economics classes.

We find little evidence of negative spillovers.
Point estimates for term GPA are positive in all but one specification, with marginally significant effects for all-classes GPA in some specifications.
\footnote{At this university, term GPA is affected only by classes taken for a letter grade.
	Hence, students may not have attrited from the sample but may have taken all courses Pass/No Pass and thus have no term GPA.
	As such, we report sample sizes for each GPA specification.
}
In our worst-case specification, the 95\% confidence interval rules out negative effects on grades in other courses (Term GPA excluding Micro A) larger than -0.06 on a 4.0 scale, about 2\% of the control mean.
Effects on classes passed are small, insignificant, and directionally positive.
Treatment students were somewhat less likely to take Micro A for a letter grade, but this difference is only marginally significant in one specification.
We find no effect on the fraction of classes taken Pass/No Pass.

\subsection{Spillovers to other study methods in Micro A}\label{subsec:spillovers_studying}

We next examine whether treatment caused students to substitute away from other study methods within Micro A.
In Table~\ref{tab:spillover_studying}, we estimate the effect of treatment on class attendance, discussion board engagement, and tutoring lab visits.
We find no statistically significant effects on any of these margins.
Point estimates for class attendance and tutoring visits are near zero, and discussion board activity is directionally positive.
We do not observe all study methods, for example reading the textbook or doing problem sets, and so cannot rule out substitution along these unobserved margins.
However, the evidence taken together is consistent with treatment having increased total study time: treatment did not reduce the study activities we observe within Micro A, nor did it affect students' broader course-taking behavior or performance in courses take in the same quarter.
Since watching videos takes time, the additional hours must have come at least in part from non-academic activities such as leisure or time in the labor market.

\subsection{Spillovers to subsequent term}\label{subsec:spillovers_micro_b}

All students in Micro B maintained access to the IMVH and were encouraged to watch videos, but no grade incentive for video watching was offered.
We can therefore test whether the behavioral change induced by the \textit{Incentive} in Micro A persists after exogenous incentives are removed.

In Table~\ref{tab:spillover_100b}, we find that treatment reduced the likelihood of continuing to Micro B the next quarter by 7 to 8 percentage points, though this effect is not statistically significant at conventional levels.
We find no significant effects of treatment on the number of classes passed or withdrawn in the subsequent quarter.
The negative point estimate on Micro B continuation is surprising given that treatment improved exam performance in Micro A.
We conjecture that grade neutralization in our experimental design may explain this result: treated students who increased their effort and earned higher exam scores but saw no corresponding increase in their letter grade in Micro A may have been demoralized and less likely to continue to Micro B (see Section~\ref{sec:gradeneutralization} for further discussion).

In Table~\ref{tab:microb_results}, we address selection into Micro B using three approaches: post-double-selection estimates on the selected sample as a benchmark (Column 1), generalized Lee bounds following \textcite{semenova2025} (Column 2), and inverse probability weighting on baseline covariates (Column 3).
The generalized Lee bounds relax the standard monotonicity assumption of \textcite{lee2009} to conditional monotonicity, and we condition on baseline covariates following \textcite{heiler2024} to tighten the bounds.
Conditional monotonicity is untestable and may not hold even within covariate subgroups.
We therefore present generalized Lee bounds as one approach to addressing selection into Micro B, and interpret the results as suggestive rather than definitive.
The inverse probability weighting results should also be treated with caution, as they correct for selection on observables but cannot address unobserved demoralization or other latent selection channels.

Treated students watched substantially more videos in Micro B: 8.3 more unique videos and 1.3 more hours of unique content than control students (Column 1).
While the PDS and IPW estimates for video watching are statistically significant, the generalized Lee bound confidence interval includes zero.
We therefore interpret the persistence of video watching in Micro B as suggestive evidence.
Point estimates for Micro B exam scores are positive, 0.12 standard deviations on the final exam, but confidence intervals are wide.

\subsection{Treatment Effect Heterogeneity}\label{subsec:heterogeneity}

Table~\ref{tab:het_table} presents estimates of $\beta_2$ from Equation~\ref{eq:het_spec}.
We find no significant heterogeneity across our blocking variables, year and first midterm score, consistent with stable treatment effects across year in the experiment and across the distribution of student abilities (who scored below the median on the first midterm.)
In Appendix Figures~\ref{fig:locallin_mid2} and~\ref{fig:locallin_final}, local linear regressions show no significant differences along the midterm 1 score distribution, though for the final exam, point estimates are the lowest for the lowest-scoring students.
We find no significant heterogeneity by pretreatment video watching, except for a marginally significant positive effect on the second midterm for unique videos.

We find no significant heterogeneity by transfer status.
For gender, we find a marginally significant negative interaction for female students on the final exam, suggesting that male students may have benefited more from the intervention.
For ethnicity, we find significant negative effects for Asian students and positive effects for White students on the second midterm, but no effects on the final exam.
However, after adjusting for multiplicity, either using a Bonferroni correction or the less conservative methods proposed by \textcite{lsx2019}, none of these results remain significant.

\section{Models of Studying Behavior} \label{sec:models}

To interpret our results, we consider three models of student studying behavior and their predictions for how students respond to a grade incentive to encourage use of a time-consuming educational input, such as instructional videos.
\footnote{For all three models, we do not address the issue that the IMVH is a relatively unique study tool in that, to our knowledge, it is the first instructional book to be created entirely of videos.
	However, given the availability of close substitutes to the IMVH (lecture capture, for example) we do not explore the added issues of inducing students to use a study tool whose usefulness is not known to the instructor.
}
In all three models, students allocate time across an incentivized study input, $v$, an unincentivized study input, $o$, and leisure.
The grade incentive is earned by consuming at least $T$ units of $v$.
We focus on predictions for \textit{compliers}, the students induced by the incentive to increase their consumption of $v$.

In a \textit{neoclassical} model, students know the returns to all study methods and allocate time optimally.
The grade incentive distorts this allocation, drawing students away from their first-best mix of $v$ and $o$.
This model predicts bunching at the threshold $T$, since compliers prefer to spend marginal hours on leisure or the other study method.
If total study time remains constant or falls, exam performance should decline because study time is now suboptimally allocated.
Importantly, students return to baseline behavior once the incentive is removed.

In an \textit{imperfect information} model, students do not know the returns to all study methods and may under invest in effective ones.
The incentive induces students to try studying using $v$, and as they do, they update their beliefs about the returns.
This model does not predict bunching at $T$ as students who discover $v$ is more effective than they previously believed will continue using $v$ beyond the threshold.
A key prediction is that use of $v$ will remain elevated in subsequent courses, even without incentives, as students have learned an effective study tool.

In a \textit{behavioral/procrastination} model, students plan to study more than they actually do when the time comes, consistent with two-self models in which a "planner'' self desires high grades while a "doer'' self prefers immediate leisure.
The incentive helps students follow through on study plans up to $T$.
This model predicts bunching at the threshold and, in the absence of the incentive, a return to baseline behavior.

We summarize the predictions of the three models for our experiment in Table~\ref{tab:modelpredictions}.
Two predictions distinguish the models: whether video watching persists in subsequent courses without incentives, and whether students bunch at the 40-video threshold.
We find suggestive evidence that treatment students continued watching significantly more videos in Micro B when no grade incentive was offered, and as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:hist_incent}, treatment students typically watched more than 40 videos rather than bunching at the threshold.
These patterns are consistent with the imperfect information model, though they do not rule out alternatives.
The absence of bunching is suggestive but not decisive, as students did not receive immediate feedback on their video counts and may have rationally overshot the threshold under any model, particularly given the steep grade penalty for falling short of 40 videos.
\footnote{Though, anecdotally, many students reported keeping track by checking off a list or taking notes, suggesting at least some students were aware of their approximate count.}
Similarly, persistence in Micro B is consistent with belief updating but also with habit formation.\footnote{The welfare implications differ: under imperfect information, students have discovered an effective study method and are making better-informed choices, while under habit formation, continued video watching may not reflect an optimal allocation of study time.}

\section{Discussion} \label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Grade neutralization and downstream selection}
\label{sec:gradeneutralization}
A central design choice in our experiment was to curve \textit{Incentive} students' letter grades to match the distribution of \textit{Control} students, ensuring that final letter grades would not vary by treatment arm.
This decision was motivated by fairness, but it has implications for downstream outcomes that has not been fully appreciated in the pedagogy field-experiment literature.

In our experiment, treatment increased both study effort and exam performance.
Grade neutralization means that these effort and performance gains are not reflected in letter grades, which creates two opposing effects on treated students' decisions to continue to Micro B.
The improved exam performance reflects greater mastery of the material and likely increased students' confidence about succeeding in the next class, which would increase the likelihood of taking Micro B.
But the increased effort without any letter grade benefit may lead students to conclude that Micro A is a harder course than they thought, or that the return to effort in economics classes is lower than they believed, which would reduce the likelihood of taking Micro B.

Any pedagogy intervention that uses grade neutralization and finds that treatment increases both effort and performance will face this same kind of tension: the very combination of results that indicate the intervention worked also generates ambiguous selection into downstream outcomes.
This ambiguity has a direct econometric implication.
Standard Lee (2009) bounds require that treatment either weakly increases or weakly decreases selection for all units.
Our design plausibly violates this: the same treatment that makes some students more likely to continue, through improved exam performance and updated beliefs about the returns to studying, simultaneously makes others less likely to continue, through the disconnect between effort and letter grade.
We address this selection in Section~\ref{subsec:spillovers_micro_b} using generalized Lee bounds of \textcite{semenova2025} and inverse probability weighting, but each relies on untestable assumptions.

Future experiments using grade neutralization could improve downstream inference by eliciting students' intentions to enroll in subsequent courses before treatment assignment.
Such a measure would tighten bounds-based approaches by defining subgroups within which the direction of selection is more likely to be uniform, and would strengthen reweighting methods by directly capturing a key determinant of downstream enrollment.

\subsection{Experiment design choices and \textit{local} treatment effects} \label{subsec:lates_section}

Our LATE estimates are \textit{local} to compliers, and several features of our experimental design influence who complies and how they watch videos, which in turn affects the estimated LATE relative to the population ATE.

First, the composition of videos watched differs across arms.
In Figure~\ref{fig:video_cdf}, control group watch rates vary widely across videos, from nearly 0\% to over 70\%.
Treatment and control watch rates are positively correlated across videos: videos that control students find valuable, treatment students also watch at high rates.
However, the incentive sharply increases watch rates for videos that few control students watch: treatment watch rates are 70\% or greater for many videos whose control watch rates are between 5\% and 20\%.
Because earning the incentive required watching 40 of the 48 eligible videos, and as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:hist_incent} nearly 60\% of treatment students watched all 48, these high treatment watch rates are largely mechanical and do not reveal whether content or time costs drive students' choices.
Nevertheless, these are the marginal videos induced by treatment.
If they are less valuable per hour, the LATE is diluted relative to the population ATE, while if they are equally valuable, the composition effect is not a concern.

Second, the timing of video watching matters.
In Figure~\ref{fig:video_cdf_week}, control group watch rates taper off towards the end of the term while treatment watch rates remain high, consistent with the incentive serving as a commitment device to spend more time studying for the final exam.
However, the single end-of term deadline also left room for procrastination.
As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:hist_binge}, binge-watching was more prevalent among treatment students.
Research suggests that spreading learning over time is more effective than cramming ~\parencite{kornell2009}, and \textcite{aw2002} find that externally set deadlines improve grades.
The greater prevalence of binge-watching before the final exam relative to the second midterm likely contributes to smaller final exam LATEs.

Collectively, it is not obvious how the population ATE compares with our estimated LATEs.
Neoclassical economic theory suggests that always-takers benefit more from videos than compliers, since always-takers were already watching without the incentive, revealing that the returns justify the time cost.
This implies the ATE exceeds the LATE.
However, under the imperfect information model, always-takers may simply have had better prior information about the return to video watching rather than higher returns.
Our intuition is that the population ATE is likely closer to the second midterm LATE, which is less affected by binge-watching and video composition effects, than to the final exam LATE.
Offering multiple deadlines throughout the term, rather than a single end-of-term deadline, may reduce the gap between the second midterm and final exam LATEs.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{subsec:limitations}

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our results.
First, the population studied is students who scored below the median on the first midterm of an intermediate microeconomics course at a large, highly-selective public research university.
The extent to which treatment effects vary by course, instructor, university, or along the top half of the midterm score distribution is important but beyond the scope of this paper.
Additionally, grading standards in the course we study are stringent.
A small grade incentive is less likely to be effective in courses with substantial grade inflation, where the marginal value of a few grade points is lower because most students already expect high grades.

Second, the positive effects we estimate are attributable to watching IMVH videos and spending more time studying for the course.
Our experimental design does not allow us to separately identify these two mechanisms.
However, in \textcite{afg2025}, grade incentives for completing out-of-class work were randomized within each of five classes of introductory and intermediate microeconomics at the same university, including the same course one year prior to our experiment.
Despite incentivizing online quizzes instead of video watching in intermediate microeconomics, applying the incentive to all students rather than only those below the median on the first midterm, and placing a larger grade weight on the incentivized activity (8 percentage points versus 4), that study estimates the incentive increased quiz-taking by 59 percentage points and finds an ITT effect on the final exam of 0.14 standard deviations in the most comparable course section, nearly identical to our final exam ITT of 0.18 standard deviations.
That study also finds suggestive evidence that effects are larger for lower-performing students, consistent with our decision to target below-median students where returns are likely to be greatest.
The similar effect size across different out-of-class activities, grade weights, student populations, and randomization strategies is consistent with the conclusion in \textcite{afg2025} that learning gains come from increased study time rather than the specific activity incentivized, and that small grade incentives are an effective way to encourage out-of-class engagement.

Third, we do not directly observe total study time.
Our conclusion that the incentive increased total study time rests on the indirect evidence that other observed study activities did not decline and that watching videos takes time.
A pre- and post-treatment time-use survey would provide direct evidence on this margin and allow researchers to quantify the total time cost of the intervention.

\section{Conclusion} \label{sec:conclusion}

We conduct a within-class field experiment to estimate the effect of introducing a small grade incentive for watching instructional videos on exam performance.  
The grade incentive for video-watching was obtained by reducing the grade weight on the already-completed first midterm.
Assigning four percent of the course grade to watching videos motivated below-median students to watch 63 percent more unique videos by the final exam.
This induced increase in video-watching improved their exam performance by approximately 0.18 standard deviations.
Using treatment assignment as an instrument for video watching, we estimate that each incentive-induced hour of unique video content is associated with a 0.16 to 0.18 standard deviation increase in second midterm scores.
This is our preferred LATE estimate, as it is less attenuated by the binge-watching that compressed final exam LATEs.
We find no evidence that treatment reduced engagement with other observed study methods or harmed performance in concurrent courses, consistent with the incentive increasing total study time rather than reallocating it.
Our LATE estimates therefore likely capture the combined effect of video watching and additional study time.
However, treatment students were somewhat less likely to enroll in the subsequent course in the term following the experiment, possibly because the incentive design prevented higher exam scores from translating into higher grades.

We find patterns consistent with an imperfect information model of learning.
Treated students frequently watched more than the 40 videos required to earn the grade incentive, and there is suggestive evidence that they continued watching at higher rates in the following course when no incentive was offered, consistent with students updating their beliefs about the returns to studying.

A prior experiment incentivizing quizzes in the same course produced nearly identical effect sizes, which implies that learning gains come primarily from increased study time rather than the specific activity.
Our findings suggest that small grade incentives can counteract students' underestimation of the returns to studying and may generate lasting behavioral change.

\subsection{Additional experiment details}\label{subsec:additional_details}

\subsubsection{Randomization} \label{subsubsec:a_randomization}
Students were assigned to treatment arms using a matched pairs design, a special case of blocked randomization in which each block contains exactly two units, one treated and one control.
Several authors detail how matched pair designs can improve the \textit{ex ante} precision of treatment effect estimates (versus complete randomization) by matching treatment units whose potential outcomes are similar (e.g.~\cite{ir2015,ai2017}).

We were unable to observe most pretreatment covariates until after the experiment had concluded because of student privacy considerations, making it impossible to block on these variables.
We learned from the previous cohorts' data that between the first midterm score and math quiz score, both observable at the time of randomization, the midterm score predicted significantly more variation in the final exam score.
Hence, we stratified on first midterm score when assigning treatment.
While we could have used an alternative method (e.g.\ matching methods) that take into consideration multiple covariates when assigning treatment, we opted for a simpler design given the high correlation between midterm and math quiz score and the comparatively high number of missing observations for the latter assessment (the math quiz was given on the second class day, before some students had enrolled).

Shortly after issuing the first midterm exam grades, We assigned treatment.
In both years, treatment assignment happened after the "drop without penalty" deadline on Friday of the fourth week.
We ordered the students by exam score, then paired students along this ordering for those below the median.
Within pairs, we randomly assigned one student to \textit{Incentive}, the other to \textit{Control}.
By construction, these two arms were \textit{ex ante} balanced on first midterm score, and we verified at time of treatment that the arms were also balanced on math quiz score.
Since randomization was performed independently across year cohorts, by construction, the samples were also balanced on year.

\subsubsection{Selection of control variables} \label{subsubsec:a_selection}

As discussed in Section~\ref{subsec:empiricalstrat}, we always include first midterm score and year as controls, following the recommendation of \textcite{bm2009} to control for all covariates used to seek balance when assigning treatment.
For additional controls, we use the post-double-selection (PDS) procedure of \textcite{bch2014a} to avoid the researcher degrees of freedom associated with hand-picking covariates~\parencite{sns2011}.

PDS is a two step process.
In the first step, both the outcome of interest (e.g., videos watched) and treatment status are predicted separately using lasso regression, which shrinks coefficient estimates towards zero.
Since treatment is randomly assigned, the lasso should shrink most, if not all, of the coefficients towards zero when predicting treatment status.
The union of all covariates with non-zero coefficients across the two-step lasso regressions, i.e., those that predict the outcome, those that predict treatment status, or both, is then included as controls in the second step, where treatment effects are estimated.
To ensure that midterm score and year are always included, we partialed out these controls from the first-step prediction models by residualizing both sides of the equation as described in \textcite{bch2014b}.

In Table~\ref{tab:controlvars_desc}, we describe all candidate covariates.
In Table~\ref{tab:controlvars_selected_itt}, we report covariates selected for each outcome.

\subsection{LATE estimators using Neyman's repeated sampling approach} \label{subsec:a_neyman_late}

In this section we derive LATE estimators using the repeated sampling approach of \textcite{neyman1923}, which considers each pair as an independent, completely randomized experiment.

Similar to a Wald estimator, the point estimate of the LATE is the mean within-pair difference in outcome divided by the mean within-pair difference in videos:

\begin{equation} \label{eq:neyman_late_spec}
	\wh{\gamma} = \frac{\wb{\Delta y}}{\wb{\Delta v}} = \frac{\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J} \Delta y_j }{\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J} \Delta v_j} = \frac{\wb{y_I} - \wb{y_C}}{\wb{v_I} - \wb{v_C}}
\end{equation}

where $y$ is the outcome of interest (grades) and $v$ is the number of videos, both indexed by pair $j\in J$ and treatment status $C$ or $I$ for \textit{Control} or \textit{Incentive}, respectively.

We use the delta method to calculate the approximate standard error of $\wh{\gamma}$.
First, we define the following normally-distributed random variables:
\begin{equation}
	\begin{split}
		Y = \wb{y_I} - \wb{y_C} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_Y, \sigma^2_Y) \\
		V = \wb{v_I} - \wb{v_C} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_V, \sigma^2_V)
	\end{split}\label{eq:equation1}
\end{equation}

Using a first-order Taylor expansion and letting $g() = \frac{Y}{V}$, we have:
\begin{equation}
	\begin{split}
		\text{Var}(g) & = \operatorname{E}[(g - \operatorname{E}(g))^2]                                                                                           \\
		              & \approx \operatorname{E}[(g(\theta) + (Y-\theta_Y)g'_Y(\theta) + (V-\theta_V)g'_V(\theta) - g(\theta))^2]                                 \\
		              & = \operatorname{E}[(Y-\theta_Y)^2 (g'_Y(\theta))^2 + (V-\theta_V)^2 (g'_V(\theta))^2 + 2(Y-\theta_Y)(V-\theta_V)g'_Y(\theta)g'_V(\theta)] \\
		              & = \text{Var}(Y)(g'_Y(\theta))^2 + \text{Var}(V)(g'_V(\theta))^2 + 2\text{Cov}(Y,V)g'_Y(\theta)g'_V(\theta)
	\end{split}\label{eq:equation2}
\end{equation}

Expanding about $\theta=(\theta_Y,\theta_V)=(\mu_Y,\mu_V)$ and letting $g'_Y(\theta) = \mu_V^{-1}$ and $g'_V(\theta) = \frac{-\mu_Y}{\mu_V^{2}}$:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:var_expanded}
	\begin{split}
		\text{Var}(g) & \approx \frac{1}{\mu_V^2}\text{Var}(Y) + \frac{\mu_Y^2}{\mu_V^4}\text{Var}(V) + 2\frac{-\mu_Y}{\mu_V^{3}}\text{Cov}(Y,V)  \\
		              & = \frac{\mu_Y^2}{\mu_V^2}(\frac{\sigma_Y^2}{\mu_Y^2} + \frac{\sigma_V^2}{\mu_V^2} - 2\frac{\text{Cov}(Y,V)}{\mu_Y \mu_V})
	\end{split}
\end{equation}

%We use the following variance estimators of $Y$ and $V$ from Equation~\ref{eq:se_imai}:
\begin{equation}
	\begin{split}
		\wh{\text{Var}(Y)}   & = \wh{\sigma_Y^2} = \frac{1}{J(J-1)}\sum_{j=1}^{J} (\Delta y_j - \wb{\Delta y})^2                            \\
		\wh{\text{Var}(V)}   & = \wh{\sigma_V^2} = \frac{1}{J(J-1)}\sum_{j=1}^{J} (\Delta v_j - \wb{\Delta v})^2                            \\
		\wh{\text{Cov}(Y,V)} & = \wh{\sigma_{YV}} = \frac{1}{J(J-1)}\sum_{j=1}^{J} (\Delta y_j - \wb{\Delta y})(\Delta v_j - \wb{\Delta v})
	\end{split}\label{eq:equation3}
\end{equation}

and the following estimators for the population means of $Y$ and $V$:
\begin{equation}
	\begin{split}
		\wh{\mu_Y} & = \wb{\Delta y} \\
		\wh{\mu_V} & = \wb{\Delta v}
	\end{split}\label{eq:equation4}
\end{equation}

Substituting these variance and means estimators into the final step of~\ref{eq:var_expanded}, we arrive at the standard error estimator for $\wh{\gamma}$:

\begin{equation}
	\wh{\sigma_{\gamma}} = \frac{\wb{\Delta y}}{\wb{\Delta v}} \sqrt{ \frac{\wh{\sigma_Y^2}}{\wb{\Delta y}^2} + \frac{\wh{\sigma_V^2}}{\wb{\Delta v}^2} - 2 \frac{\wh{\sigma_{YV}}}{\wb{\Delta y} \wb{\Delta v}} }\label{eq:equation5}
\end{equation}
